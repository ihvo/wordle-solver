Stage 0 · The game

First, how Wordle works

You guess a secret 5-letter word. After each guess the game paints every letter a color. That's all the information you get — so good play is really about reading the colors.

Try it. Type any 5-letter guess and a secret answer, and see how Wordle would color your guess:

Green = right letter, right spot. Yellow = right letter, wrong spot. Gray = the letter isn't in the word (or you've already found all of its copies).

Everything below is built on this one rule. A computer can do this coloring for every possible word in a flash — which is exactly what makes the next part possible.

Stage 1 · The teacher

A math expert that never loses

Before any AI, we built a classic, rock-solid Wordle player using pure math. Think of it as a patient expert who, every turn, asks one question: "which guess will cross out the most words?"

The key idea — the "still-possible" list. At any moment, only some words are still consistent with the colors you've seen. Call that the candidate list. A great guess is one whose colors will chop that list down the fastest. (Mathematicians call the size of the cut "information" — but you can just picture the list shrinking.)

Stage 2 · The clone

Teaching a net to copy the expert

We showed a small neural net thousands of the expert's moves and said, in effect, "copy this." Learning by imitation like this is called behavior cloning. Simple idea — but what we showed the net turned out to be everything.

The mistake first. We gave the net the history of past guesses and colors and asked it to pick the next word. It learned almost nothing — it played about as well as guessing at random.

Why it failed: from a raw list of past moves, the net would have to re-figure-out the whole game from scratch every turn. Too much to ask.

The fix that made it work. Don't make the net reconstruct the game — hand it a tidy summary of the still-possible list. The most useful summary is just which letters are common, and where. Below are the most common letters in each position among the words still standing. This little picture is (almost) all the net needs to pick a smart guess.

Taller green bar = that letter shows up more often in that position among the remaining words. The net reads these bars (as numbers) and learns which word to play.

Stage 3 · Shrink it

The fancy part wasn't needed

Our first net used attention — the same heavy machinery behind chatbots. We tried ripping it out. The net played exactly the same. So we kept the smaller one.

Why? Once you hand the net the still-possible-list summary, there's nothing left for the fancy machinery to figure out. The net is basically a ranker: "given these remaining words, which is the best guess?" A plain, small net does that fine. Smaller is better when it's free.
Stage 4 · The one weakness

The trap: too many look-alikes

Our copy-cat net had one blind spot. Near the end of some games, a cluster of words differ by a single letter — and it would try them one at a time and run out of turns.

You're stuck on a classic trap. The answer is one of these, and you have a limited number of guesses left:

The trick is called "probing". Instead of gambling on look-alikes one by one, you play a totally different word — one you know is wrong — purely because its colors tell the different look-alikes apart in a single move. The expert does this naturally. The copy-cat net never could.

We even tried to teach probing by example — and failed.

We relabeled the training data to show the net the expert's probe moves. It got worse, at every size we tried. The reason is subtle: a probe's whole value is a setup for the next move. You can't capture "this is a good setup" in a single snapshot label. That failure is the clue for the final stage.

One free win along the way: the very first guess is always the same situation, so we just fixed it to a strong opener (SLATE). That alone nudged the average from 3.62 → 3.55 at no cost.

Stage 5 · Reinforcement learning → 100%

Let it play, and reward winning

If you can't show the net the trick, let it discover the trick. We had it play huge numbers of games and gave it a reward for winning. Over time it learned to probe on its own. This is reinforcement learning (RL).

Why this works where copying didn't. A reward for winning the whole game naturally values a probe — because the payoff shows up a move later. A single "copy this" label never could. The lesson in one line: probing is a reward problem, not a copying problem.

The recipe, in plain terms

1

Reward winning

+ points for solving, more for solving faster. Every win beats every loss — so it never trades a sure win for a risky shortcut.

2

Show it a few expert escapes

On the hardest traps it might never stumble onto a probe by luck. So we slip in the expert's winning move now and then, just to point the way.

3

A safety rule

If the few remaining words fit in your leftover guesses, just guess one — you can't lose. Only probe when truly stuck. All the learning focuses on that one decision.

4

An "anchor" so it doesn't forget

While learning the new trick, gently hold its everyday play steady. Skip this and it learns probes but forgets the basics.

The climb to 100%

Win rate as training went on. It wobbles while exploring, then locks onto the probing trick and tops out at a perfect score.

An honest bump in the road. One version hit a great score with the safety rule but quietly became terrible without it — it had over-focused on traps and forgotten normal play. The "anchor" (step 4) fixed it. Lesson: always watch the number you're not optimizing.
